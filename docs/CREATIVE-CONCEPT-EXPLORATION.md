## 1. Purpose

Explore distinct visual territories for Gerhana without treating a popular style, illustration genre, color palette, or animation technique as the brand.

The durable identity remains **Irvan Gerhana — Product Engineer**: a product-minded systems builder who connects product thinking, interface craft, software engineering, and production concerns to turn complex work into useful digital products.

The visual system must help visitors understand that combination and trust the evidence. It must not make the visitor decode a visual metaphor before learning who Irvan is.

This document prepares comparable concept studies. It does not authorize UI implementation, public-copy changes, or selection of a final art direction.

## 3. Concept territories

Four directions are intentionally different in their visual grammar. The names are working labels for discussion, not public brand language.

---

## Concept A — The System Atlas

**Thesis:** A good product makes a complex territory navigable.

### Core metaphor

The visual field behaves like an abstract atlas of connected systems: flows, layers, boundaries, routes, and relationships. The composition suggests the act of understanding a complex problem and finding a clear path through it.

This is not a map of a real city, a literal architecture diagram, or a dashboard.

### Hero composition

- Editorial headline occupies a calm, high-contrast region on the left.
- A large, spatial visual field occupies the right and extends beyond the viewport edge.
- Thin routes and larger structural forms connect several distinct zones.
- One visually dominant path moves from a fragmented/ambiguous region toward a clear, resolved structure.
- Small-scale detail appears only where it helps the viewer understand the larger composition.
- The visual field should feel dimensional through layering and overlap, not through a generic floating 3D object.

### Visual grammar

- Topographic contours, path lines, boundaries, nodes, and layered planes.
- Clear hierarchy between primary route, secondary connections, and ambient detail.
- Controlled asymmetry and generous negative space.
- A small number of accent signals rather than many competing colors.
- Typography acts like an editorial title, not map labels scattered around the screen.

### Motion potential

- A route is revealed as the visual field resolves.
- Layers align or separate to expose a relationship.
- One focused movement guides attention toward the next section.
- No continuous animated network, floating data points, or random node pulsing.

### Site-wide extension

- Work index: each case study can be situated in a domain or workflow without turning the page into a literal map.
- Case studies: show the key route through context, decision, implementation, and current state.
- Approach: expose the decision path and trade-offs.
- Experience: connect roles to relevant domains and evidence.
- Writing: editorial markers can reference a topic or thread.

### Risks to test

- Can easily look like generic SaaS data visualization or a network-technology landing page.
- Too many nodes/labels can make it busy and technical for its own sake.
- The visual metaphor may imply system diagrams even where the project is primarily about human workflow and interaction.

### Proof test

Show a person who has never met Irvan the hero for five seconds. Ask what kind of work he does. If the answer is only “data visualization” or “network engineer,” the concept is over-literal and needs refinement.

## Concept B — The Editorial Instrument

**Thesis:** The quality of the work is revealed through judgment, structure, and detail.

### Core metaphor

The website behaves like a carefully composed independent technical journal or design publication. Typography, rhythm, precise layout, and deliberate use of material create the signature. The website does not need a large illustration to feel distinctive.

This is not a generic minimalist portfolio or a replica of a magazine template.

### Hero composition

- Headline and brand line are the dominant visual event.
- Use strong typographic scale and an unusual but disciplined relationship between title, role, and copy.
- A small number of carefully composed visual artifacts may appear: a crop, a technical annotation, a diagram fragment, or a material detail.
- The visual signature comes from composition and typographic rhythm rather than a large decorative object.
- A strong transition into the work section can make the first real project evidence the next visual event.

### Visual grammar

- Strong baseline and column grids.
- Distinctive display/body/technical type roles.
- Editorial crop, whitespace, page rhythm, dividers, and restrained annotations.
- A consistent but non-repetitive index/numbering language.
- Material contrast may be used, but colors and typefaces remain to be chosen.

### Motion potential

- Typography reveals through a single coherent transition.
- Editorial planes or crop windows shift to introduce the work.
- Interaction states feel precise and immediate.
- No elaborate scene or lengthy intro is required.

### Site-wide extension

- Case studies benefit from a strong reading system and clear evidence hierarchy.
- Résumé can remain document-like while sharing typographic decisions.
- Writing becomes a natural extension of the editorial identity.
- Experience and capability sections can use timeline and column structures without card grids.

### Risks to test

- Can become too quiet, generic, or indistinguishable from a common minimal portfolio.
- If the typography is the only signature, it may not express the product/system dimension strongly enough.
- An overly editorial approach can make the interface feel static or impersonal.

### Proof test

Remove the logo and brand name. Does the composition still have a recognizable point of view? Then inspect one case study: does the same system make technical depth easier to understand, or does it merely decorate text?

## Concept C — Material to Meaning

**Thesis:** Complex work becomes useful when raw material is shaped into a coherent product.

### Core metaphor

A generative material transformation: fragments, signals, components, or irregular forms gradually acquire structure and meaning. The material can be pixel-based, typographic, diagrammatic, photographic, or entirely new. Pixel/dither is one possible technique, not the concept.

### Hero composition

- Headline remains clear and readable in a stable editorial region.
- A single large material composition carries the visual story.
- The material begins with variation and irregularity, then forms a deliberate structure.
- The transformation should connect to a real relationship—e.g. raw context into workflow, workflow into interface, interface into a working system.
- The composition must have a strong static resolved state; animation cannot be required to make it legible.

### Visual grammar

- Material contrast, density, grain, fragments, repetition, alignment, and transformation.
- One dominant form with a clear direction of change.
- More expressive than the Editorial Instrument, but more disciplined than ambient generative art.
- A distinct graphic signature can be created without locking into one image genre.

### Motion potential

- One focused transformation from ambiguity to clarity.
- A small number of layers change state in a readable sequence.
- The final form resolves and rests.
- Pointer response, if used, is optional and subtle.
- Reduced-motion mode shows the resolved composition immediately.

### Site-wide extension

- Section transitions can reuse the idea of material becoming structured, but should not repeat the same morph in every section.
- Case-study pages can use selected material studies as chapter markers, not as substitutes for product evidence.
- Project diagrams and evidence can adopt a controlled fragment-to-structure language.

### Risks to test

- This is close to previous pixel/dither experiments; it may reproduce the old visual identity under a new name.
- The transformation can become decorative and fail to explain the actual professional proposition.
- It can be visually memorable in the hero but difficult to extend to content-heavy sections.

### Proof test

Replace the hero art with a real case-study visual. Does the page retain a coherent identity? If the concept collapses without its generative hero artwork, it is not yet a complete design system.

## Concept D — The Product Field Notes

**Thesis:** The most convincing identity is the evidence of how a product is understood, shaped, built, and operated.

### Core metaphor

A field notebook for product engineering: observations, workflow fragments, interface details, implementation decisions, architecture boundaries, operational notes, and project evidence are organized into a coherent visual narrative.

This is not a literal scrapbook, desk, wall of sticky notes, or fake project documentation.

### Hero composition

- Strong editorial headline and identity lead the first impression.
- A curated composition of a few different artifacts supports the story: one workflow fragment, one interface crop or abstract interaction study, and one system/operational detail.
- The artifacts should feel like selected evidence from a working process, not floating dashboard cards.
- The visual relationship between artifacts shows how decisions at one layer affect another.
- Where no public artifact is approved, use clearly labelled conceptual studies rather than fabricating a shipped product screenshot.

### Visual grammar

- Evidence-led composition, crop windows, document-like surfaces, linework, annotations, and clear captions.
- Distinct visual roles for real evidence, conceptual illustration, and explanatory diagrams.
- Typographic hierarchy that supports scanning and deeper reading.
- Warmth may come from material and editorial tone, not from a literal studio scene.
- The design can accommodate both light, document-oriented content and darker, expressive hero work if contrast and transition are coherent.

### Motion potential

- Artifacts are introduced as a sequence of connected ideas.
- A visual crop can reveal context; a diagram can resolve into an implementation detail.
- Motion helps explain relationships between artifacts.
- No floating card carousel or random parallax.

### Site-wide extension

- Selected work is the natural center of the visual system.
- Each case study can show context, role, workflow, decisions, implementation, and current status.
- Expertise can be connected to actual evidence.
- Experience can connect professional roles to projects where disclosure permits.
- Writing can look like published field notes, keeping the personal thinking layer connected to the work.

### Risks to test

- Depends on approved project evidence; current public portfolio contains only one approved, self-referential case study.
- Without real evidence, the concept can become a collage of invented artifacts.
- Too many annotations and crops can become cluttered.
- Generated conceptual visuals must never be mistaken for real product screenshots or client deliverables.

### Proof test

Build one hero and one case-study opening using only approved/publicly cleared material. If the concept needs invented client dashboards or fake product screens to look complete, reject or substantially simplify it.

## 4. Cross-concept comparison

This comparison is qualitative and provisional. It is not a design score or selection decision; no comparable rendered styleframes have been produced yet.

| Criterion | A — System Atlas | B — Editorial Instrument | C — Material to Meaning | D — Product Field Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identity expression | Strong systems lens | Strong craft/judgment lens | Strong transformation metaphor | Strong end-to-end work lens |
| First-visit clarity | Risk of being read as data/network tech | Very high if copy leads | Depends on transformation clarity | High if artifacts are real and labelled |
| Memorability | Spatial visual language | Typographic/compositional signature | Generative transformation | Evidence composition |
| Evidence compatibility | Good for workflows and architecture | Strong for deep reading | Needs a real evidence layer | Strongest, but depends on disclosure |
| Full-site extension | Workflows, case studies, approach | Homepage, CV, writing, case studies | Transitions and visual studies | Work index, case studies, experience, writing |
| Main risk | Generic data-viz aesthetic | Too quiet/generic | Becomes decoration or repeats dither | Fake or cluttered evidence collage |

### Recommended study order

Create comparable static frames for **A, B, and D first**. Keep C as an expressive challenger, because earlier exploration already leaned heavily on pixel/dither transformations.

This is a recommendation for what to test, not a selection of the final identity.

## 5. Evaluation protocol

Use the weights from the Brand Identity Roadmap:

| Criterion | Weight | Evaluation question |
|---|---:|---|
| Identity fit | 25% | Does it express Product Engineer and the product-minded systems-builder identity? |
| Clarity | 20% | Can visitors understand the person and value quickly? |
| Memorability | 20% | Does it have a distinctive point of view without copying a trend? |
| Evidence compatibility | 15% | Can real work and project detail remain central? |
| Extensibility | 10% | Can it support all sections and future project stories? |
| Accessibility/performance | 10% | Can it work across devices and reduced-motion settings? |

For each concept:
1. Use the exact same copy and target viewport.
2. Create a static hero composition first; do not use motion to hide hierarchy problems.
3. Include the top of the next section.
4. Create a mobile counterpart for the two strongest concepts.
5. Place one concept next to an actual approved case-study opening.
6. Ask five-second comprehension questions: Who is this? What does he build? What makes the approach different? Where can I see evidence?
7. Record risks, trade-offs, and owner feedback.
8. Do not select a winner from the score alone.

## 6. Shared art-direction constraints

Every concept must respect these identity requirements:

- The public role remains Product Engineer.
- “From pixel to production.” remains the approved brand line.
- Product thinking, interface craft, engineering, and production concerns must be connected—not shown as four unrelated service cards.
- The headline and primary action must be readable immediately.
- The hero must remain understandable in a static state.
- One dominant visual idea per section.
- No unnecessary eyebrow labels, pseudo-HUDs, badge collections, floating panels, or decorative technical text.
- Generated imagery is conceptual, never product evidence.
- No client/project details may be inferred from private or draft sources.
- Avoid recognizable copied game/film characters, logos, worlds, or proprietary visual assets.
- The identity must survive a change of palette, typeface, illustration style, and motion treatment.
