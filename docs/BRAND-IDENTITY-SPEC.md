## 1. Purpose

This specification defines the durable identity and intended experience of uigerhana.space. It is deliberately **not** a visual trend brief.

Anime, cyberpunk, urban environments, literal workspaces, pixel art, Bayer dithering, 3D illustration, and cinematic motion are possible visual treatments—not the identity itself. They may be selected later when they express the brand and improve the experience. None is mandatory.

**Governing principle: Gerhana must be larger than any visual style currently used to express it.**

This document is the source of truth for identity, perception, experience, verbal expression, visual principles, and motion. The roadmap lives in `docs/BRAND-IDENTITY-PLAN.md`.

### 2.2 Identity thesis

**Irvan Gerhana is a product-minded systems builder who connects product thinking, interface craft, software engineering, and production concerns to turn complex work into useful digital products.**

“Product-minded systems builder” is an internal identity descriptor, not a replacement job title.

A recurring pattern across the work and project discussions is:

1. Understand a problem in its operational and business context.
2. Make workflows, information, decisions, and constraints understandable.
3. Shape a usable product experience around that logic.
4. Engineer the software and systems behind the experience.
5. Consider how the product is deployed, secured, monitored, maintained, and improved.

This describes the working approach. It does not claim that every project included every stage or that every project was delivered end-to-end. Public project stories must state only contributions and project states supported by evidence.

### 2.3 Brand promise

**Make complex work clearer through thoughtful products and production-minded engineering.**

This is a strategic promise, not a quantified outcome guarantee. Specific project claims still require evidence.

## 3. Identity attributes

| Attribute | Meaning in practice | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Product-minded | Starts with people, workflows, business logic, constraints, and intended outcomes. | Feature-first implementation without context. |
| Thoughtful | Makes deliberate decisions and can explain trade-offs. | Over-explaining or unnecessary complexity. |
| Cross-disciplinary | Connects product, UX, software, data, and operations. | Claiming every discipline on every project. |
| Craft-conscious | Cares about interaction details, code quality, consistency, and delivery. | Perfectionism that obscures priorities. |
| Systems-aware | Understands architecture, boundaries, integrations, security, reliability, and maintenance. | Technical spectacle without purpose. |
| Practical | Values useful, operable software and real evidence. | Inflated promises and abstract innovation language. |
| Curious and experimental | Explores tools when they serve a real problem. | Making novelty or trends the identity. |

The differentiator is the combination of business context, interface and interaction quality, hands-on implementation, system architecture, and production awareness—not a list of frameworks.

### 3.1 Working principles

1. **Start with the workflow.** Understand people, decisions, data, and constraints before choosing an implementation.
2. **Make complexity legible.** Use clear information architecture, canonical data models, and thoughtful interfaces.
3. **Build AI with context.** Treat AI as part of a larger system with business context, governance, integration boundaries, and explainable behavior.
4. **Think beyond the interface.** Consider security, deployment, monitoring, maintainability, and operational behavior as product concerns.

These principles do not imply that every case study demonstrates all four.

## 4. Perception specification

### 4.1 Primary audience

Potential clients and business partners should conclude that Irvan:
- Understands complex business and operational workflows.
- Reasons about the product, not only implementation tickets.
- Cares about how people experience software.
- Can work across interfaces, application logic, data, and system concerns.
- Considers whether software can be operated and maintained.
- Makes grounded claims and explains his contribution.

### 4.2 Secondary audience

Professional peers, recruiters, and hiring managers should find:
- A clear Product Engineer identity.
- The breadth and context of his experience.
- Relevant capabilities and technical depth.
- Accurate career history and responsibilities.
- Selected project evidence and writing.
- A usable résumé and clear contact route.

All audiences must encounter one coherent identity. Do not create contradictory personal, agency, freelancer, or studio personas.

### 4.3 Desired impressions

A successful visit should leave five impressions:

1. **Product-aware:** understands why a workflow or feature exists.
2. **Design-sensitive:** cares about usability, hierarchy, interface quality, and detail.
3. **Technically grounded:** can explain and build the systems behind an experience.
4. **Production-minded:** considers operation, reliability, security, and maintenance.
5. **Trustworthy:** distinguishes proven work from experiments, plans, and unverified outcomes.

Do not optimize the site merely for “impressive animation,” “trendy anime portfolio,” “many frameworks,” or “agency with a service catalogue.” Visual quality earns attention; clarity and evidence earn trust.

## 5. Experience specification

### 5.1 Overall experience

The site is a personal professional platform, not an agency funnel. It should feel clear before clever, human without fabricated biography, expressive without noise, technically credible without excluding non-specialists, and coherent from introduction through work, experience, writing, and contact.

### 5.2 Visitor journey

| Stage | Visitor question | Required experience |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Who is this person? | Name, Product Engineer positioning, concise value proposition. |
| Relevance | What problems does he work on? | Explanation of product, design, engineering, and systems scope. |
| Evidence | What has he actually contributed to? | Selected, approved stories with clear role and status. |
| Method | How does he think and work? | Principles, decisions, constraints, and process. |
| Depth | What is his background? | Experience, capabilities, and writing. |
| Connection | What is the next step? | Clear contact path and résumé access. |

Visitors must not need to understand a visual metaphor before understanding the professional identity.

### 5.3 Homepage narrative

1. **Introduction / Hero** — identify Irvan as Product Engineer; explain the connection between product thinking, interface craft, and software engineering; retain the brand line; offer selected-work and résumé routes; use one memorable visual idea.
2. **How the work connects** — explain the sequence of understanding a workflow, shaping an experience, engineering a system, and operating it.
3. **Selected work** — lead with a small set of approved, evidence-backed stories.
4. **Capabilities** — group capabilities by the problems and product layers they support; technologies are supporting evidence, not the main story.
5. **Experience** — present résumé-backed roles accurately, including overlapping dates.
6. **Principles and writing** — show how Irvan reasons about product, systems, engineering, and delivery; use verified public writing.
7. **Contact** — make the next step direct and professional; expose only approved contact channels.

Existing sections may be retained or reorganized where evidence and usability justify it.

### 5.4 Interaction behavior

- Navigation and key actions must be clear on desktop and mobile.
- The primary action must be understandable without animation.
- Hover feedback should be subtle; keyboard focus must remain visible.
- Menus, selects, popovers, and controls need consistent behavior and spacing.
- No interaction may depend on hover alone.
- Do not hide essential information behind hover, scroll-triggered reveals, or decorative controls.
- Avoid unnecessary cards, floating pills, badges, and decorative labels.
- Do not add eyebrow labels by default; use them only when they provide real orientation.

## 6. Visual identity principles

### 6.1 Identity before style

The brand is defined by meaning, verbal identity, content, and experience—not by illustration trends or animation techniques. A concept must remain recognizable if its palette, imagery, and motion treatment are replaced.

The working phrase **“The Art of Building”** may guide exploration internally; it is not yet a public tagline or a requirement to use literal craft imagery.

### 6.2 Visual character

Balance four qualities:

- **Expressive:** a recognizable point of view and memorable composition.
- **Structured:** strong hierarchy, grid logic, rhythm, and consistency.
- **Playful:** room for experimentation and discovery.
- **Technical:** precision, clarity, and respect for the subject.

### 6.3 Composition and hierarchy

- The person and professional proposition are the first-order message.
- Use one dominant visual idea per major section.
- Give typography and content enough room to be read.
- Use negative space intentionally.
- Make the relationship between sections deliberate.
- Do not sacrifice headline or CTA legibility for dramatic artwork.
- Avoid density that competes with the work itself.

### 6.4 Color and typography

The owner selected **Manrope + Plus Jakarta Sans** for the System Atlas direction: Manrope for display/headings and Plus Jakarta Sans for body/interface. The owner also selected GSAP as the motion engine, with the existing GSAP/ScrollTrigger stack retained.

The palette is delegated to design and proposed as **Basalt / Limestone / Sea Glass / Signal Copper**. The base layout gutter is **1rem**, with rem-based spacing and component-scoped `--py` / `--px` inset pairs. Full roles, contrast checks, spacing tokens, and implementation examples are in `docs/design-system/SYSTEM-ATLAS-ART-DIRECTION.md`.

These are selected art-direction inputs and a design proposal, not a permanent production token freeze. They still require visual review across the responsive frames and the approval gates in the roadmap. The visual system must support contrast, reading comfort, hierarchy, responsive use, and consistent application across the homepage, case studies, résumé, and supporting pages.

### 6.5 Imagery and signature elements

- Choose imagery because it expresses an identity attribute or explains real work.
- Prefer distinctive art direction over stock developer-workspace imagery.
- Generated illustration is atmospheric or conceptual unless verified as a real project artifact.
- Never present generated visuals as product screenshots, production UI, client work, or implementation evidence.
- Avoid literal code, dashboards, servers, or circuit boards when they add no information.
- Do not copy recognizable characters, logos, locations, or proprietary visual assets.
- A signature element—typography, composition, motif, material, or interaction—must connect to the brand, be repeatable, and work across content types.
- Pixel/dither may remain part of the visual history, but must not be forced into every section or treated as the only interpretation of “From pixel to production.”

## 7. Motion and interaction principles

Motion is a communication tool, not proof of sophistication.

- Animate a meaningful change of state, relationship, or focus.
- Prefer a few coherent sequences over many disconnected effects.
- Make content understandable before and after animation.
- Avoid long intros, mandatory preloaders, scroll locks, or pinned journeys that delay content.
- Avoid constant background movement.
- Treat pointer response as optional enhancement.
- Provide touch-equivalent access.
- Honor `prefers-reduced-motion` with an intentional static or low-motion state.
- Keep effects performant and lifecycle-safe.
- Never make animation the only way to discover information.

For the owner-selected System Atlas direction, GSAP is the motion engine. Use one coherent route-draw timeline and restrained waypoint reveals; the static route and all content remain fully available without JavaScript. Do not introduce pinned/locked scrolling, perpetual background movement, or motion-dependent content. See `docs/design-system/SYSTEM-ATLAS-ART-DIRECTION.md` for the initial choreography and reduced-motion rules.

## 8. Voice and editorial system

### Voice
Clear, grounded, thoughtful, human, technically literate, confident without inflated superlatives, curious, and specific.

### Writing rules
- Prefer concrete verbs and plain explanations.
- Use first-person singular for Irvan's own work.
- Distinguish personal contribution from team delivery.
- Distinguish shipped work, active development, prototype, experiment, and roadmap target.
- Explain the problem before naming a framework.
- Use factual claims supported by the résumé, approved repository evidence, verified public links, or explicit owner decisions.
- Avoid “world-class,” “industry-leading,” “revolutionary,” and similar claims without defensible proof.
- Do not invent testimonials, metrics, clients, credentials, or launch outcomes.

## 9. Content integrity and evidence

Follow the source precedence in the Master Implementation Brief:
1. Latest owner-approved identity decisions.
2. Owner-provided résumé for résumé facts, subject to disclosure.
3. Existing repository for current implementation facts.
4. Prior project context as discovery input, not automatic publication approval.
5. Editorial copy as proposed language until owner-approved.

| Evidence state | Public behavior |
|---|---|
| Approved case study | May render with approved evidence and copy. |
| Draft / review required | Excluded from public selectors and detail routes. |
| Private source / disclosure pending | Generalize or omit; never reveal private identifiers. |
| Prototype / experiment | Label explicitly; do not imply production release. |
| Roadmap / target | Describe as planned, not implemented. |

Past discussions about ERP/POS, SaaS, AI platforms, analytics, and service-management software help explain the breadth of work. They do not establish public ownership, release state, client attribution, outcomes, or publication permission. Use the existing review process before publishing.

A case study should state the applicable context, problem, user/workflow, project status, role, contribution, team boundary, decisions, constraints, deliverables, outcomes, verified technology, and approved artifacts. A build passing is not proof of deployment or business impact.

## 10. Brand guardrails

**Always:** lead with Product Engineer; connect product, interface, engineering, and operations; make work and reasoning visible; favor clarity and evidence; keep sections coherent; respect attention; remain independent of visual trends.

**Never by default:** build a generic agency page; use anime/cyberpunk/game UI/a literal studio as an identity substitute; use generic developer desks, floating dashboards, server cutaways, or abstract 3D without purpose; add decorative HUDs or badges to fill space; turn every capability into a card; make the first screen a motion demo; present generated visuals as project evidence.

These are defaults, not bans. A future approved concept may use an expressive treatment when its purpose is clear.

## 11. Accessibility, performance, and responsive requirements

- Semantic landmarks and heading hierarchy.
- Keyboard-operable navigation and actions.
- Visible keyboard focus even when hover is subtle.
- Contrast and text-scaling checks.
- Equivalent content if canvas or animation is unavailable.
- Reduced-motion support.
- Touch interaction without hover dependency.
- No unnecessary client rendering or continuous offscreen animation.
- Optimized/deferred non-critical imagery.
- Testing across mobile, tablet, laptop, and wide desktop.
- Meaningful content order at every breakpoint.
- Usable state if animation is disabled, assets fail, or JavaScript is delayed.
